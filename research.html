  • Can Speed Cameras Make Streets Safer? Quasi-Experimental Evidence from New York City with Jonathan Ben-Menachem and Brenden Beck Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122(50), 2025 [Article] [PDF] [Repository]
    Abstract

    Each year, approximately 40,000 people die in vehicle collisions in the United States, generating $340 billion in economic costs. To make roads safer without expanding police contact, many cities have turned to automated traffic enforcement, cameras that detect and fine speeding motorists. Does automated enforcement reduce vehicle collisions and injuries? Many previous studies are limited to correlational evidence. By contrast, this study estimates the causal effect of automated enforcement on road safety in a difference-in-differences design. We exploit the staggered rollout of 2,000 speed cameras across New York City between 2014 and 2023, combining data on 700,000 collisions and 200,000 injuries with data on 18 million tickets issued. We find that cameras reduce collisions and injuries by 5 and 2.5% per month on average, respectively. Cumulatively, over the seven months following their introduction, collisions declined by 30% and injuries by 16%.

  • When Policing Technology is Discontinued: 911 Response and Crime After Chicago’s ShotSpotter Decommission with David Hackett Under Review
    Abstract

    ShotSpotter, a gunshot detection technology used by police to locate and respond to gunfire, has been the subject of fierce debate in cities around the country over whether to cancel existing contracts amid questions about its efficacy. While prior research has evaluated ShotSpotter’s adoption, causal evidence on the effects of its discontinuation is limited. This paper evaluates Chicago’s September 2024 cancellation of ShotSpotter using a difference-in-differences design with district-by-calendar-month fixed effects. In the year following ShotSpotter’s decommissioning, the 911 system handled reports of gunfire faster: dispatch and on-scene arrival times for shots-fired calls fell when measured from the first report dispatchers received, which before cancellation was often a ShotSpotter alert. But the system also lost these earlier alerts, and when measured from the resident’s call, dispatch and on-scene arrival times did not detectably change. There is suggestive evidence of faster dispatch for non-gunfire Priority 1 calls. Shootings and homicides did not detectably increase. Alerts with no accompanying 911 call seldom led to evidence of a crime: 5% ended in a case report, compared with 34.6% of alerts accompanied by a citizen gunfire call. These findings suggest that Chicago saw modest improvements in how quickly the 911 system handled reports of gunfire, with no detectable change in response times for residents who called and no detectable increase in gun violence after cancellation.
